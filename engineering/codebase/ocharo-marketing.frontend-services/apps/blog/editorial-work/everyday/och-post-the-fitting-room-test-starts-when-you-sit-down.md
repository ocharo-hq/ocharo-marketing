# The fitting-room test starts when you sit down

*Last updated: 2026-09-27 12:07 AM*

## Editorial draft

The mirror shows a moment. A few ordinary movements can reveal whether a garment belongs in your day.

**Reader promise:** Build a movement-based fitting-room routine around the activities a garment must support.

**Format:** Try-on field guide

## Manuscript

The trousers pass the mirror test. Then you sit down. Picture the waistband, pockets, and back rise suddenly becoming the main conversation. The front looked neat; the length worked with your shoes. Those observations still count. Choosing clothes also deserves a small rehearsal of being a person in them: sitting, reaching, carrying something, and getting on with the day.

### 1. Rehearse the intended day

Start with the intended day. For a desk-day outfit, rehearse a few minutes at a chair. For a layer you expect to wear to dinner, think about sitting at a table and taking it off again. Pick two or three actions you will actually perform. The most revealing test may be as ordinary as reaching for your bag on the next chair.

Sit in an ordinary position, stand again, and reach forward. For a jacket, try carrying the bag that will accompany it. Include the layer you usually wear underneath when possible. Button it as you would at home. A garment can feel quite different with a collar, sleeve, or waistband underneath it, so give that part of the outfit a turn in the rehearsal.

### 2. Watch your hands

Pay attention to what your hands do. Do you keep pulling the hem down, pushing a sleeve back, or moving a fastening away from your skin? One adjustment does not settle anything. A repeated adjustment points to a question worth answering while you still have the garment in front of you.

### 3. Let the garment settle

Try the action again without arranging everything immediately afterwards. Where does the garment settle? Does that position still feel and look acceptable to you? Clothes do not have to remain frozen in their mirror pose. The useful distinction is between movement you welcome and movement you will spend the day correcting. Your own comfort and preferred appearance set that boundary.

### 4. Ask two different questions

Ask two separate questions. Can I do what I need to do comfortably? Do I like the shape during and after those movements? You may be happy with the space through the back but dislike where the hem ends when you sit. Name both things. It is easier to discuss one specific change than a general feeling that the garment is almost right.

### 5. Keep a different cut in the conversation

Suppose the roomier size gives you an easier reach but a shoulder line you dislike. Try another cut if one is available, then repeat the same movement. If you are considering an alteration, ask a maker who can examine the garment what is feasible. Bring the movement into that conversation too: show where the sleeve catches or the front pulls, rather than describing it from memory.

### Take this with you

Add two ordinary movements to your next try-on, and note every adjustment you repeat.

### One question to keep

What everyday movement should your favourite type of garment be tested for?
