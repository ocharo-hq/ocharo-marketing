# Two sizes, neither quite right

*Last updated: 2026-09-27 12:07 AM*

## Editorial draft

When choosing a size becomes a stalemate, describe the fit problem before deciding what to change.

**Reader promise:** Replace an unhelpful size-label debate with observations a seller or maker can respond to.

**Format:** Fit problem-solving worksheet

## Manuscript

Suppose one size solves the shoulder question and the other solves the front. Neither completes the shirt. Choosing a winner would mean accepting sleeves you keep pushing away or a front that feels crowded. Before settling, describe what each version gets right and wrong. You will have a much better conversation about the garment when the problem has a location and a movement attached to it.

### Where is the problem, and when does it appear?

Start with the garment rather than a verdict about your body. Name the area: shoulder, neckline, chest, sleeve, waist, hip, or length. Add the action that makes the problem noticeable. 'The sleeve catches when I bend my elbow' is more informative than 'The fit is strange'. It also gives you a way to repeat the test.

### What should stay exactly as it is?

Describe what works with equal care. Perhaps the hem ends where you like it, the neckline lies as intended, or the waistband feels comfortable while seated. Keeping those successes in view prevents a vague search for a completely different garment when only one feature is bothering you. It also shows what a proposed change should preserve.

### Which garment is a useful reference?

Choose a garment you already wear for a similar purpose. Lay both out, if available, and compare the parts relevant to your question. A favourite loose shirt is not automatically a useful reference for a fitted jacket. The reference earns its place by doing the job you want the new piece to do.

If discussing measurements remotely, agree on how they are taken. Ask whether a number measures the body or the garment, and which points define it. Write the method beside the number so you can repeat it with your reference piece. Then compare the part you are concerned about, such as the sleeve opening or the distance across the back. A label alone cannot show you that relationship.

### Which options are real?

You have three possible routes: wear one version as it is, ask about an alteration, or try a different cut. For the alteration route, show the maker what you want to change and what you want to keep. Ask them to examine the construction before deciding. Shortening a sleeve may affect a cuff; changing a length may move a detail. The specific garment needs the specific conversation.

Sometimes neither version answers the question. Keep your notes and try them against another garment. If the sleeve catches during a reach, repeat that reach in the next fitting. If the neckline was exactly right, remember what you liked about it. You are building a useful description of your preference, not collecting a list of wrong sizes. The next conversation can begin where this one became interesting.

### Take this with you

Record the location, triggering movement, and desired result before choosing a size or discussing alterations.

### One question to keep

What fit detail would you keep exactly as it is while changing something else?
