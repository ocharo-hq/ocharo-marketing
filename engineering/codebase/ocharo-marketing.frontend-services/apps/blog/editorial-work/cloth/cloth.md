## What made the previous article feel like an advertisement

The previous cloth article began with a product visualisation, described the featured cape, and concluded with material questions for an inquiry. It gave the reader more ways to assess an item, but little independent reason to return to a publication. Its short sections had similar imperatives and lacked an explanatory tension or a particular historical reference.

The revised set gives each reader an object of curiosity: a wavering edge, a missing maker, a care mark, a join that changes a repeat, or a broad word such as handmade. It supplies a bounded explanation or an original exercise. None of the manuscripts sends the reader to a purchase or contact action. A post remains useful when its reader owns no Ocharo garment.

## Editorial voice

- Open with a small puzzle rather than a declaration of product value.
- Explain unfamiliar terms at the point of use.
- Use concrete actions: trace, compare, sketch, separate, and reread.
- Attribute documented historical or technical facts to their institution.
- Mark visual readings as the viewer’s response rather than the maker’s intention.
- Keep unknowns specific. An absent reverse view leaves one question; it does not require a general disclaimer.
- End with a question that extends the reading rather than asks for an inquiry.

| A timed game | A five-minute looking routine delays the label until minute four. | A remembered detail and a distinction between looking and learning from a record. |
| A claim unpacked | Handmade is expanded into specific stages and people. | Better questions without unsupported claims about quality or impact. |

The posts share an editorial ethic, but vary their organising device. Three are exercises, two focus on interpretation of records, and the others explain methods or familiar claims. There are no fabricated conversations, visits, quotations, or anecdotes. The opening scenarios invite the reader to imagine or perform an action; they do not describe an event that happened to the author.

## Cultural and factual scope

- Uzbek references concern particular recorded objects and Margilan safeguarding activity. They do not establish Ocharo materials, suppliers, training, or production methods.
- The Lampung tapis example belongs to a Sumatra context and is explicitly separated from Uzbek garments.
- The phulkari exercise retains the V&A’s Panjab context. It is a specific comparison reference, not a claim about Uzbek embroidery.
- The term ikat connects a technical method; the Indonesian comparison is not used to transfer ceremonial meanings.
- Regional cloth names are not treated as sufficient evidence of fibre percentages, geographical production, or care.
- Museum qualifiers such as probably and present-day remain attached to the attribution.
- Historical textile records are not descriptions of how everybody in a region dressed.
- The care article decodes general categories. It gives no treatment instruction for an unidentified Ocharo piece. It links to the current GINETEX guide rather than reproducing care-symbol artwork.

## Images and article presentation

Typographic covers fit all ten articles. Their question-shaped titles provide variety without repeating a product mannequin image. Historical/process imagery, if added later, should be attached to its precise collection or institution with its own rights assessment and descriptive caption. The current generated garment visuals are not evidence of historical weaving, fibre content, or workshop activity.

Suggested reading cues: show the format and reader promise near the introduction; render a quiet takeaway after the essay; retain the closing question; place source links beside the factual section. Avoid repeated brand-contact cards inside articles. A source section can be concise, with the label and support scope available to the reader.
