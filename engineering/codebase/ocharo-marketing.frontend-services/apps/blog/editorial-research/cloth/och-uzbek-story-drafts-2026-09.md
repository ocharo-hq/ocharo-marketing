## Five widths, one brilliant Bukhara room

**Place:** Bukhara\
**Format:** Cloth story\
**Reader promise:** See how cloth, pattern, and the space around them work together in one Bukhara hanging.\
**Proposed slug:** `bukhara-five-widths-one-room`

A nineteenth-century hanging reveals how narrow lengths of cloth can fill a room with colour.

![Bukhara ikat hanging with red and cream chevrons beside elongated diamond and oval shapes.](reference-images/och-bukhara-pardah-2006-152.jpg)

Wall hanging (pardah), Bukhara, 1850–75. Cleveland Museum of Art, gift of Guido Goldman, 2006.152. Open access. [Collection record](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/2006.152). See the [asset register](och-reference-assets-2026-09.json) for rights and original download URLs.

The red arrives first. Then the diagonals begin to climb, pale lines interrupt the dark, and small flashes of blue pull your eye sideways. This Bukhara hanging seems restless even on a screen. Stay with it for a moment and another feature emerges: its different patterns are neighbours, meeting along long, narrow joins.

### A room-sized composition

The Cleveland Museum of Art dates this pardah to 1850–75. Five loom widths make its front: silk threads run lengthways, crossed by cotton. The reverse has a printed-cotton lining. The museum places such hangings in the reception rooms of Central Asia’s urban elite. Here, fabric helped shape the room itself.

Imagine the difference between seeing a folded length on a shelf and meeting this whole expanse on a wall. A motif that fits beneath your hand becomes part of your surroundings. The eye can travel through it while the body stays still.

Further reading: [Cleveland Museum of Art — Wall hanging (pardah), 2006.152](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/2006.152).

### The joins change the rhythm

Look near the middle, where the diagonal bands gather into repeated peaks. Follow them towards the right and the movement changes: elongated shapes stack above one another, followed by a narrower strip of rounded forms. The cloth does not settle into one repeated gesture across its entire width.

Those changes make the joins worth noticing. Try covering one side of the image with your hand. The remaining piece has a different balance. Uncover it and the neighbouring pattern changes how the centre feels again. Arrangement is doing as much work as any individual shape.

### Bukhara had more than one silk story

The Russian Museum of Ethnography describes a wider Bukhara textile world that included silk shoyi, mixed silk-and-cotton adras, and silk velvet called bakhmal. It also records polishing shoyi and adras to heighten their sheen. Different surfaces gave colour different ways to appear.

That variety invites another kind of looking. A pattern has scale and direction, but its material also meets the light. Imagine the same red beside a window, across a fold, or on a soft velvet surface. The colour stays in the conversation; its voice changes.

Further reading: [Russian Museum of Ethnography — Silk Fabrics of Bukhara and Samarqand](https://ethnomuseum.ru/en/resources/silks-in-the-culture-of-the-peoples-of-eurasia/silk-fabrics-of-bukhara-and-samarqand/).

### Let the whole cloth return

Pull back from the details. The narrow joins recede, and the hanging becomes one confident field again. Its pleasure lies in that exchange: a room-filling composition becomes several lengths of cloth, then becomes a room-filling composition once more.

### Takeaway

The placement of neighbouring patterns can give a textile as much character as the motifs themselves.

## A Samarkand shop, stacked with colour

**Place:** Samarkand\
**Format:** Archive encounter\
**Reader promise:** Explore a historic Samarkand fabric shop through its visible details and regional textile context.\
**Proposed slug:** `samarkand-a-shop-full-of-cloth`

One early-twentieth-century photograph opens a view into a city’s textile world, fold by fold.

![A seated Samarkand fabric merchant surrounded by stacked cloth inside a wooden shop doorway.](reference-images/och-samarkand-fabric-merchant.jpg)

Fabric merchant, Samarkand, between 1905 and 1915. Sergei Mikhailovich Prokudin-Gorskii; Library of Congress. Digital colour composite by Blaise Agüera y Arcas, 2004. [Collection record](https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/2018680238/). See the [asset register](och-reference-assets-2026-09.json) for rights and original download URLs.

The doorway is barely wider than the display. Cloth rises behind the seated merchant in close horizontal layers: pale edges, dark bands, small repeating marks, a sudden stretch of red. Above him, the stacks disappear into shadow. Before you identify a single fabric, the photograph gives you the pleasure of choosing where to look.

### A whole shop in a single frame

Sergei Mikhailovich Prokudin-Gorskii photographed this Samarkand merchant between 1905 and 1915. The Library of Congress identifies silk, cotton, wool fabrics, and carpets in the display. Its catalogue brings several textile worlds into the same small shop; the photograph lets you linger among them.

Start at the threshold. The patterned floor covering reaches towards the street, while the wooden doors frame the darker interior. Cloth occupies the space behind the merchant almost like another wall. You can see how much has been fitted into the depth of the opening.

Further reading: [Library of Congress — Fabric merchant, Samarkand](https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/2018680238/).

### Folds offer a different view of pattern

An unfolded textile lets a design spread out. Here, folded edges compress the view into thin bands. One length offers a line of dots, another a pale stripe; larger forms remain tucked away. The stacks create a second pattern from the many patterns they contain.

The merchant’s robe gives the eye a different experience. Its rounded forms extend across a clothed body, gathering and changing direction at the folds. Look back at the shelves: how would one of those small glimpses behave if a whole length were opened?

### Beyond the doorway

A separate Russian Museum of Ethnography exhibition includes Samarqand fabric samples dated 1900–1902, alongside regional yarn, dyes, and loom equipment. It places Samarqand and Bukhara within a history of silk weaving. The shop belongs to a city whose cloth stories also reached into workshops.

Think of those two views together: the small selection visible on a folded edge, and the many stages needed to make a length of fabric. A shop concentrates that work into choices of colour, surface, and scale. Its shelves are a starting point for following the cloth further.

Further reading: [Russian Museum of Ethnography — Silk Fabrics of Bukhara and Samarqand](https://ethnomuseum.ru/en/resources/silks-in-the-culture-of-the-peoples-of-eurasia/silk-fabrics-of-bukhara-and-samarqand/).

### The colour has its own journey

This version of the photograph is a digital colour composite made from the original glass negative’s three frames. The brilliant bands around its edge belong to that photographic image. Inside them, the wooden doorway offers another frame: a quiet rectangle filled with things to notice.

Return to one stack. Choose the folded length you would most like to see opened. Its hidden pattern is where this encounter leaves you.

Further reading: [Library of Congress — Fabric merchant, Samarkand](https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/2018680238/).

A fabric shop can reveal a textile culture through arrangement, variety, and the promise of an unfolded length.
