## Editorial purpose

Build an accessible, visually rich account of textiles in Uzbekistan: what people make, how patterns enter cloth, where particular examples come from, and how textiles participate in daily life. Bukhara and Samarqand belong in the opening stories, alongside Margilan and other documented regional practices.

This is a researched starter inventory of **20 cloth and practice entries**, not an exhaustive census of every textile name, local spelling, fibre mixture, or historical variant. The list separates things visitors often encounter together but that describe different attributes. It is a proposal for editorial coverage, not a statement of decisions by the designer.

The editorial promise: **look closely at one textile, understand something surprising, and leave with a better eye for the next one.**

## How the atlas should be organised

Use independent filters rather than forcing every term into one category:

| Filter | Examples | What the reader learns |
|---|---|---|
| Cloth or structure | Adras, atlas, velvet, striped cloth, printed cotton | What the textile is made from and how it is constructed |
| Pattern process | Warp resist dyeing, weaving stripes, printing, embroidery, patchwork | When and how the visible design appears |
| Place | Bukhara, Samarqand, Margilan, Nurata, Shahrisabz, Boysun, Karakalpakstan | Where the particular object or practice is documented |
| Pattern family | Rosettes, vines, stripes, chevrons, medallions, named animal forms | What to notice before interpreting it |
| Object or use | Robe, hanging, cover, cap, bag, carpet | How people encountered the textile |
| Time and people | Object date, maker, community, workshop, collector or researcher | Who is known and what the record leaves open |

Silk and cotton are fibres; satin is a weave; ikat is a resist-dye process; adras is a cloth category; suzani is an embroidered textile category; chapan is a garment. Give every entry a visible category label so the atlas teaches these distinctions naturally.

Keep Uzbekistan as the geographic editorial focus while naming Uzbek, Tajik, Karakalpak and other documented communities accurately. Present-day borders are not a shortcut to exclusive ownership of historical practices.

## Evidence labels

- **A — object or direct practice record:** a museum catalogue, national heritage record, or identified practitioner supports the precise claim.
- **B — collection scholarship:** a museum-based scholarly or cultural-heritage publication supports the category; definitions may need object-level refinement.
- **C — research lead:** a term is documented, but the available page does not establish enough detail for a standalone technical explainer.

These indicate editorial readiness, not cultural value. An entry may have strong evidence that a name existed and weak evidence about its exact weave or regional distribution.

## Pattern families: teach the eye before supplying a meaning

| Family | A documented starting point | Engaging treatment | Interpretive boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stripes | Bekasam's coloured warp in the State Museum account | Compare spacing and rhythm using two labelled samples | Stripe width alone does not identify a town |
| Amulet forms and chevrons | CMA Bukhara hanging 2006.152 | Let readers locate three amulet-patterned widths and two inner chevron widths | Say the museum identifies amulet designs; do not invent the owner's beliefs |
| Medallions and floral forms in abr cloth | Bukhara examples in the State Museum account | Isolate one repeat, then reveal the whole cloth | A common shape may have different names across records |
| Rosettes, palmettes and winding stems | Met Bukhara suzani 447384 | Compare a dense border with a more open centre | Describe this attributed object's composition, not an unchanging regional rule |
| Large dark-red rosettes with dark curls | Samarkand examples in the History of Religion collection account | Show how a few large shapes can organise an entire room | Locate the claim in the collection and period studied. [Source](https://society.uz/news/detail/news/670) |
| Floral sprays | CMA suzani 2016.89, probably Shahrisabz, 1800–1850 | Trace one stem to its branching flowers | Keep the museum's “probably”; Shahrisabz is not Samarkand city. [Source](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/2016.89) |
| Horn and household forms | Karakalpak carpet names: paired horns, comb and amulet | Pair a locally recorded name with an exact photographed motif | Identify Karakalpak sources rather than treating these as a single Uzbek symbol dictionary. [Source](https://society.uz/news/detail/news/260) |
| Geometry in carpets | Rhombi, triangles and stepped outlines in the Samarkand collection account | Ask which shapes survive a change in scale | A collection in Samarkand does not establish production in Samarkand. [Source](https://society.uz/news/detail/news/473) |
| Designs transferred between media | Tashkent factory textiles adapted embroidery and architectural decoration | Compare a textile design with its documented source motif | Trace a documented borrowing rather than implying all similar shapes share one origin. [Source](https://sanat.orexca.com/2012/2012-4/the-fabrics-of-the-tashkent-textile-factory-the-collection-of-the-state-fine-arts-museum-of-uzbekistan/) |

Names such as *tirikamon*, *bahor* and *chaman* are recorded in the State Museum account. Treat them as named designs or colour descriptions within that record. Do not convert every red patch into “love,” every pomegranate into a universal fertility claim, or every blue thread into a spiritual code. Reader curiosity can be sustained by competing interpretations when their sources are named.

## Two opening stories with documented source material

### 1. Bukhara: “Five strips, one dazzling room”

**Opening question:** What changes when you stop seeing one pattern and start seeing five separate widths?

**Anchor:** *Wall hanging (pardah)*, Bukhara, 1850–75. Cleveland Museum of Art, accession **2006.152**, Gift of Guido Goldman. Museum metadata records warp ikat with silk warp and cotton weft, five panels, and a two-panel printed-cotton lining. The catalogue distinguishes three widths with amulet designs from two inner widths anticipating later chevrons. [Object record](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/2006.152)

**Proposed visual sequence:** whole object → reader locates seams → optional overlay numbers the five widths → one repeat is isolated → an original three-step diagram explains bound warp, colour application and weaving → return to the hanging at room scale. These are new editorial teaching choices, not quotations from the museum.

**Reader payoff:** after one feature, a visitor can distinguish loom widths, pattern rhythm and the point at which an ikat design enters the making process. Close with a looking prompt: “Find one repeated form. Does its edge sit in exactly the same place on each return?”

**Assets and reuse:**

- [Web image](https://openaccess-cdn.clevelandart.org/2006.152/2006.152_web.jpg)
- [Print-resolution image](https://openaccess-cdn.clevelandart.org/2006.152/2006.152_print.jpg)
- [Full TIFF](https://openaccess-cdn.clevelandart.org/2006.152/2006.152_full.tif)

### 2. Samarqand: “One market stall, many kinds of cloth”

**Opening question:** If you could pause at this stall, where would your eye go first?

**Anchor:** Sergei Mikhailovich Prokudin-Gorskii, *Fabric merchant. Samarkand*. Library of Congress item **2018680238**, reproduction **LC-DIG-prokc-21725**. The catalogue dates the photograph **1905–1915**; its associated exhibition caption uses **1911**. Use the catalogue range in the principal caption. The Library identifies silk, cotton, wool and carpets in the merchant's display. [Object record](https://www.loc.gov/pictures/item/2018680238/), [exhibition context](https://www.loc.gov/exhibits/empire/work.html)

**Proposed visual sequence:** whole stall → reader chooses an area to inspect → short looking prompts about hanging, folding, pattern scale and display → a distinction between market location and manufacturing location → link into separately documented fabric entries. The reader should get a sense of a textile market as an encounter between materials, uses and people.

**Reader payoff:** a route into Uzbekistan's textile variety through a specific place and human scene. The photograph supports a Samarkand market story; identifying individual bolts as adras, atlas, imported print or locally woven cloth requires further evidence. Ask open looking questions until those identifications can be sourced.

**Assets and reuse:**

- [Colour-composite JPEG, 0.8 MB as listed by the Library](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/pnp/prokc/21700/21725v.jpg)
- [Colour-composite TIFF](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/pnp/prokc/21700/21725u.tif)
- [Alternative digital colour rendering JPEG](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/pnp/ppmsc/03900/03948v.jpg)
- Exact URLs and reproduction identifiers are listed on the catalogue page. The principal proposed image is the 2004 composite credited there to Blaise Agüera y Arcas; the alternative rendering is credited separately to Walt Frankhauser.
- Rights advisory: **“No known restrictions on publication.”** This is the Library's statement, not a CC0 licence. [Collection rights statement](https://hdl.loc.gov/loc.pnp/res.237.prok)
- Suggested credit: “Sergei Mikhailovich Prokudin-Gorskii, Fabric merchant, Samarkand, 1905–1915. Prokudin-Gorskii photograph collection, Library of Congress, Prints and Photographs Division. LC-DIG-prokc-21725; digital colour composite by Blaise Agüera y Arcas.”

**Follow-up Samarkand feature:** a photographed and locally discussed suzani. IICAS publishes Sukhareva's 2011 study of nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century embroideries from the Samarkand Museum collection. ## Editorial formats worth building

1. **One object, one discovery:** 600–900 original words, one hero image, two focused details, short source notes. A good opening format because the evidence and visual narrative stay bounded.
2. **Fabric field note:** 300–500 words, a labelled sample, a specific sensory observation, and a short making explanation. Add further specimens over time rather than claiming one swatch defines the whole category.
3. **Place story:** one workshop, market or household context with named people and dated reporting. Use a map only when the locations are known.
4. **Pattern conversation:** two or three documented examples, maker vocabulary, a question about what changes between them. Avoid a universal symbol decoder.
5. **Making sequence:** a short video or a handful of stills paired with a carefully drawn diagram. Mark explanatory diagrams as illustrations.
6. **Object to outfit:** a separate contemporary styling exercise using properly documented modern garments. Explain scale, contrast or layering; link to the relevant culture story.

Each article should contain a memorable observation, a concrete person/place/object, at least one visual that teaches, and a useful link to the next question. Reading alone should deliver value. Product links belong where they answer an already established reader interest.

## Research checks before expanding

- Reconcile Uzbek, Russian and English terminology with a knowledgeable local textile practitioner. Several translated pages use imprecise fibre and weave vocabulary; one English inventory even translates cotton categories as “paper fabrics.”
- Build named-fabric entries around identified specimens. Museum object metadata is stronger than an undated shop category for historical claims.
- Preserve “attributed to,” “probably,” and date ranges in article captions.
- Source contemporary practice through dated interviews and demonstrations. A nineteenth-century object does not establish today's workshop methods.
- Keep maker names, pronunciation, captions, sources and image credit next to each asset in the content model.
- Ask permission for new interviews and workshop photography when obtaining them; no outreach has been sent during this task.
- Do not use Smithsonian **S2004.65** as a cleared asset: the archive gives conflicting rights statements, and its current [object page](https://asia.si.edu/explore-art-culture/collections/search/edanmdm%3Afsg_S2004.65/) says usage conditions apply.
